01 · Before you read

How the printed sheet works

Part A is the same for all three levels

Three rules

  1. The extracts are in order, but paragraphs have been left out between them. The line in italics before each extract tells you what happened in the gap.
  2. Every extract has a number (①–⑨). Use that number when a question asks you to use evidence.
  3. The small grey numbers in the margin run 1–33 through the whole extract. Use them to point at an exact line — the answer key uses the same numbers.

In this lesson

Read each extract aloud, then take one question at a time. Students answer from their chairs before anyone opens the answer check.

Every grey line number on the sheet is reproduced on screen, so evidence can always be pointed at.

Extract ① · Line 1

The Dursleys and the secret they are afraid of
EXTRACT ①  ·  LINE 1 OF 33  ·  The Dursleys and the secret they are afraid of

1The Dursleys had everything they wanted, but they also had a secret, and their greatest fear was that somebody would discover it. They didn’t think they could bear it if anyone found out about the Potters. Mrs Potter was Mrs Dursley’s sister, but they hadn’t met for several years; in fact, Mrs Dursley pretended she didn’t have a sister, because her sister and her good-for-nothing husband were as unDursleyish as it was possible to be. The Dursleys shuddered to think what the neighbours would say if the Potters arrived in the street. The Dursleys knew that the Potters had a small son, too, but they had never even seen him. This boy was another good reason for keeping the Potters away; they didn’t want Dudley mixing with a child like that.

HOOK
Hands up: would you ever pretend your own sister did not exist? Turn to your partner and give one reason the Dursleys might do that.
LINE 1 OF 33
Listen forsecret / greatest fear / shuddered / unDursleyish. They are not just rude — they are frightened of being connected to the Potters.

EXTRACT ②  ·  LINE 2 OF 33  ·  A cat that seems to be reading a map
What happened in the gapLater that morning, on the drive into town.

2It was on the corner of the street that he noticed the first sign of something peculiar – a cat reading a map. For a second, Mr Dursley didn’t realise what he had seen – then he jerked his head around to look again. There was a tabby cat standing on the corner of Privet Drive, but there wasn’t a map in sight. What could he have been thinking of? It must have been a trick of the light. Mr Dursley blinked and stared at the cat. It stared back. As Mr Dursley drove around the corner and up the road, he watched the cat in his mirror. It was now reading the sign that said Privet Drive – no, looking at the sign; cats couldn’t read maps or signs. Mr Dursley gave himself a little shake and put the cat out of his mind. As he drove towards town he thought of nothing except a large order of drills he was hoping to get that day.

Exercise 3 · Reading skills

Junior 2

Answer in full sentences. Say which extract you are using.

Q1
(Inference) In Extract ⑥ the writer adds: ‘even beady-eyed Mrs Dursley’. Why is she mentioned? What does she tell us about the street lamps?
Mrs Dursley notices everything. Naming her tells the reader that nobody at all could see what was happening, because every lamp was out — the magic is happening right under her window.
Q2
(Comparison) Dumbledore ‘popped out of the ground’ (Extract ⑥). The motorbike ‘fell out of the air’ (Extract ⑧). Compare the two arrivals. How are they different, and what does each one make you expect next?
Both arrivals are sudden. ‘Popped out of the ground’ is quiet and amusing; ‘fell out of the air’ is loud and dangerous, one sentence after ‘it swelled to a roar’. The first makes us curious, the second tells us something serious has happened.
Q3
(Inference) In Extract ④ an old man hugs a complete stranger. In Extract ⑧ Dumbledore says, ‘Scars can come in useful.’ What do these two moments tell you about how the wizards see the world?
Both show someone treating what ordinary people would avoid as a reason to celebrate: the old man hugs the stranger who knocked him over, and Dumbledore keeps a scar because it is useful. The wizards do not measure things the way the Dursleys do.

Exercise 3 · Author's craft

Junior 3

Answer in full sentences. Comment on how the writer achieves the effect as well as what the effect is.

Q1
(Structure) Extract ⑨ ends with Privet Drive described as ‘the very last place you would expect astonishing things to happen’ — yet astonishing things have just happened there. What is the effect of ending the chapter on that line?
The line returns the reader to the ordinary street after the magic, and the irony (it is exactly where astonishing things have happened) closes the chapter on a quiet joke. It also leaves the Dursleys in place for the rest of the book.
Q2
(Description) In Extract ⑧ Hagrid is described before the reader is told who he is: ‘too big to be allowed’, ‘hands the size of dustbin lids’. Explain how this description makes the reader feel, and why that matters at this point in the story.
The description makes him threatening before it makes him safe: the reader shares Dumbledore’s relief, not Harry’s. Coming after the house was ‘almost destroyed’, the size hints that rescuing Harry was dangerous.
Part B · J3

Exercise 4 · Short answers

Junior 3

Answer in your own words, then support your answer with a short quotation.

Q1
How does the writer show — without ever stating it — that Mr Dursley already knows who the Potters are?
Through his reaction, not explanation: he ‘stopped dead’, ‘fear flooded him’, and the narrative has already told us the Potters are the Dursleys’ secret (¶1, ¶6).
Q2
Why does Dumbledore say ‘Scars can come in useful’, and what does this reveal about him?
He refuses to erase Harry’s scar even though he could. It shows he values a mark of survival over neatness, and treats damage as something worth keeping — the same values behind the celebration in Extract ④.
Q3
Hagrid reports that the house ‘was almost destroyed’. Why does the writer give this information through dialogue rather than description?
Dialogue keeps the violence off-stage and filtered through Hagrid’s understatement (‘all right’, ‘swarmin’ around’), which makes it worse and keeps the chapter’s tone light; description would turn the scene backwards into a battle.
